### GEMINI
The Reality Check Technically, yes, it is possible. Strategically? It is a bottleneck waiting to happen. If you are looking to run modern, kernel-level anti-cheat games like Valorant or Fortnite, your plan has a significant "opportunity cost" in terms of performance and stability. Here is the unfiltered breakdown of your brainstorming session: 1. The Storage Trap Installing the OS on a 120GB SATA SSD while offloading the heavy-hitter games to a separate 2TB NVMe partition is a classic "frankenstein" setup. The Bottleneck: Windows is "chatty." Even if the game files are on the NVMe, Windows will constantly be hitting that 120GB SanDisk for page files, registry checks, and shader caches. You’re taking a high-end CPU/GPU combo and tethering it to an old, likely slower SATA drive. The Partition Headache: To share that 2TB drive, you’ll have to shrink your CachyOS partition. Linux handles NTFS/exFAT fine, but Windows hates Linux file systems. You will be managing cross-platform permissions, which is a recipe for corrupted game files or "Update Loops" where the launcher can't verify the data. 2. Tiny11 vs. Anti-Cheat You are choosing Tiny11 for efficiency, but Valorant (Vanguard) and Fortnite (Easy Anti-Cheat) are notoriously invasive. Vanguard’s Sensitivity: Riot’s Vanguard looks for "irregularities" in the OS environment. While Tiny11 usually works, aggressive debloating can sometimes strip out dependencies or identity certificates that the anti-cheat expects to see. If the OS feels "too" modified, you risk a shadowban or a refusal to launch. 3. The 120GB "Squeeze" A standard Windows 11 install with basic drivers and system overhead will eat 30-40GB immediately. Add in the "temp" files and shader caches for Valorant and Fortnite, and that 120GB drive will be at 80% capacity before you even launch a match. SSDs lose significant performance once they cross the 70-80% fill mark. Strategic Recommendation If you want to reach the "next level" of system optimization without wasting time on troubleshooting drive permissions later: Don't "half-ass" the dual boot. If these games are the only reason you’re returning to Windows, give them the environment they need to actually run well. The "Clean" Path: Shrink the 2TB NVMe by 250-300GB. Install Windows (or a lightly debloated version) directly onto a new partition on that NVMe. The Result: You get the NVMe speeds for both the OS and the games. Your 120GB SanDisk can then be used as a dedicated "Scurry Drive" for Linux backups, ISO storage, or a dedicated swap partition. Are you trying to save the 2TB space because you're afraid of messing with the CachyOS partition, or do you genuinely think the 120GB drive is a "safer" silo?
