# Dad

*The man who gave me the best financial advice I ever got — and taught me that sometimes the simplest answer is the right one.*

My dad lives with me and my girlfriend in Akron. Three people, one house, figuring out life as a team. It's not the typical living arrangement, but it works. We share expenses, share space, and share the burden of getting by when money is tight.

He's my girlfriend's dad, not mine — but he's been a father figure in every way that matters.

---

## The car advice

The defining moment. I was drowning in a $385/month car payment on a Honda. Credit score around 520. Facing repossession. The kind of debt trap that feels impossible to escape — you need the car to get to work, but you can't afford the payments, and your credit is too bad to refinance.

My dad didn't suggest refinancing. Didn't suggest budgeting. Didn't suggest a loan. He said: *sell the Honda, extract the equity, buy a cheaper cash car, get out of the debt trap.*

No overthinking. No analysis paralysis. Just practical, blunt, lateral thinking. The kind of advice that cuts through all the noise and gets to the actual solution.

What I remember most isn't the words — it's the tone. He said it like it was obvious. Like he couldn't believe I hadn't already thought of it. That mattered. It made me feel like the solution was simple, which meant I could actually do it.

I listened. It worked. We did the same thing for my girlfriend's car — same situation, same solution. Get out of the trap. Stop trying to optimize a bad position. Just get out.

That's how my dad thinks. Direct. Practical. No fluff.

## The household

Living in a multigenerational household means navigating bureaucracy that wasn't designed for your situation. Ohio Form 20 (Civil Fee Waiver Affidavit) asks you to list everyone in your household and their relationship to you. What do you put for your girlfriend's dad? "Roommate"? "Landlord's father"? The relationship titles matter — getting it wrong could mean losing the fee waiver.

I spent multiple conversations trying to figure this out. My dad was part of those conversations — not because he was solving the paperwork, but because his presence in the household was the reason the paperwork was complicated.

There were moments that made it awkward. When I explained the fee waiver form to him, he looked confused — not about the form itself, but about why his living here affected my eligibility at all. He's proud. It stings a little that his presence complicates my access to help. I could see it in his face. He didn't say it, but I knew.

We also navigated questions about public benefits, household income thresholds, and how one person's income affects another's eligibility. When you're living together to survive, every dollar matters, and every form is a puzzle. The bureaucracy doesn't have a checkbox for "girlfriend's dad who moved in because we needed each other."

---

## The quiet moments

Most of my conversations that involve my dad are practical — legal forms, financial questions, household logistics. But underneath all of that is a relationship built on mutual respect and shared struggle.

He doesn't talk about feelings often. He shows up differently — by fixing things, by giving blunt advice, by making sure the dishwasher works or the car is running. I remember one evening when I was spiraling about a job application. I couldn't stop thinking about whether I'd framed my warehouse experience correctly. Dad walked in, looked at my screen, and said: "You know how to do the work. If they can't see that, it's their problem, not yours." He didn't ask for details. He just cut through the noise.

I also tried to explain my AI project to him once — the agent, the vault, the second brain. I was trying to put it in terms he'd understand. That's the challenge: how do you explain that you're building a digital version of yourself to someone who thinks in concrete, practical terms? He nodded, asked a few questions, and said "So it's like a really good filing cabinet." He wasn't wrong, technically. But it was also more than that. I couldn't find the words to make him feel the scope of it.

---

## What he's taught me

**Lateral thinking over optimization.** When you're stuck, don't try to optimize the bad position. Get out. Find a different angle. The car advice is the perfect example — everyone else was trying to make the Honda payment work. My dad said "sell it."

I find myself catching myself now. When I'm cycling through 20 options for which tool to use, I'll stop and ask: what's the lateral move here? What am I avoiding because it feels like failure? Dad's voice cuts through the noise.

**Directness.** He doesn't sugarcoat. He doesn't overthink. He sees the problem, identifies the solution, and says it plainly. I try to bring that to my own decision-making, but I'm not as good at it yet. I still spiral. I still compare 20 options when the answer is obvious. But the gap between how I react and how he'd react is getting smaller.

**Practicality over pride.** Living in a multigenerational household isn't what anyone pictures for their life. But it's what works right now. Dad doesn't let pride get in the way of practicality. That's a lesson I'm still learning. He moved in not because it was ideal, but because it was necessary. And because it's necessary, it's worth doing well.
